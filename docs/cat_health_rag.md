# Cat Health: Comprehensive Reference for RAG Systems

**Domain:** Feline Health & Veterinary Care — Domestic Cats (*Felis catus*)
**Purpose:** Question-answering and semantic retrieval on commonly searched cat health topics
**Structure:** Each section is self-contained to maximize retrieval relevance
**Disclaimer:** This document is for informational purposes only and does not replace professional veterinary advice. Always consult a licensed veterinarian for diagnosis and treatment.

---

## 1. Vomiting

**What it is:** The forceful expulsion of stomach contents through the mouth. One of the most common reasons cat owners seek veterinary advice.

**Common causes:**
- Hairballs — the most frequent cause in long-haired or heavy-grooming cats.
- Eating too fast — stomach distension triggers an immediate vomit reflex.
- Dietary indiscretion — eating something that disagrees with the stomach.
- Food allergies or intolerances.
- Parasites (roundworms, tapeworms).
- Underlying illness: kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), or cancer.

**Hairball vomit vs. illness vomit:**
- Hairball: tubular, hair-filled, often preceded by retching sounds.
- Illness: watery, yellow (bile), bloody, or containing undigested food repeatedly.

**When to see a vet:**
- Vomiting more than 2–3 times per week consistently.
- Blood in vomit (red or dark coffee-ground appearance).
- Vomiting accompanied by lethargy, weight loss, or loss of appetite.
- A single episode of vomiting a foreign object or suspected toxin ingestion.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat vomit every day?* — Frequent vomiting is not normal; likely hairballs, diet issues, or an underlying condition requiring a vet visit.
- *Why does my cat vomit yellow liquid?* — Yellow vomit is bile, usually from an empty stomach (hunger vomiting) or GI inflammation.
- *Why does my cat vomit right after eating?* — Eating too fast or a food sensitivity; try a slow-feeder bowl.
- *Is it normal for cats to vomit occasionally?* — Very occasional vomiting (once a month or less) may be normal; frequent episodes are not.

## 2. Diarrhea

**What it is:** Loose, watery, or unusually frequent stools.

**Common causes:**
- Sudden dietary change.
- Food intolerance or allergy.
- Intestinal parasites (Giardia, roundworms, coccidia).
- Bacterial infections (Salmonella, Campylobacter).
- Viral infections (panleukopenia, FeLV, FIV).
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).
- Stress (new home, new pet, travel).
- Medications, especially antibiotics.
- Hyperthyroidism or kidney disease in older cats.

**When to see a vet:**
- Diarrhea lasting more than 48 hours.
- Blood or dark tarry color in stool.
- Diarrhea combined with vomiting, lethargy, or not eating.
- Kitten with diarrhea — kittens dehydrate rapidly and require prompt care.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat have diarrhea but is acting normal?* — Likely a dietary issue or mild stress; monitor closely for 24–48 hours.
- *Why does my cat have bloody diarrhea?* — Requires immediate veterinary attention — could be parasites, infection, or serious GI disease.
- *Can I give my cat Pepto-Bismol for diarrhea?* — No. Pepto-Bismol contains salicylates which are toxic to cats.
- *What can I feed a cat with diarrhea?* — A bland diet (boiled chicken, plain white rice) or a veterinary GI formula diet for 24–48 hours.

## 3. Not Eating (Anorexia)

**What it is:** A cat that refuses food partially or completely for an abnormal period of time.

**Why it is serious in cats:** Cats that stop eating for more than 24–48 hours are at risk of hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease), a potentially fatal condition where fat accumulates in the liver as a result of caloric deprivation. This risk is especially high in overweight cats.

**Common causes:**
- Illness or pain anywhere in the body.
- Dental disease — eating hurts.
- Respiratory infection (cats won't eat what they can't smell).
- Stress, anxiety, or a change in environment.
- New food introduction the cat dislikes.
- Nausea from a systemic condition.

**When to see a vet:**
- Not eating for more than 24 hours in a kitten.
- Not eating for more than 48 hours in an adult cat.
- Any loss of appetite accompanied by other symptoms (vomiting, lethargy, weight loss).

**Common questions:**
- *Why has my cat stopped eating?* — Could be anything from stress to serious illness; a 48-hour cutoff for a vet visit is a safe rule.
- *How long can a cat go without eating?* — Physically up to 2 weeks, but liver damage (hepatic lipidosis) can begin within 2–3 days in some cats.
- *My cat won't eat dry food but will eat wet food — is that okay?* — Yes; wet food is often preferable for hydration and palatability.
- *How do I get a sick cat to eat?* — Warm the food to enhance aroma, try strong-smelling options like tuna or chicken broth (no onion/garlic), hand-feed small amounts.

## 4. Weight Loss

**What it is:** A noticeable or measurable decrease in body weight not explained by intentional dietary changes.

**Common causes:**
- Hyperthyroidism — the most common cause of weight loss in cats over 10 years old.
- Diabetes mellitus.
- Chronic kidney disease (CKD).
- Cancer.
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).
- Dental disease (pain makes eating difficult).
- Intestinal parasites.
- Depression or stress-related anorexia.

**Key distinguishing signs:**
- Weight loss + increased appetite + hyperactivity → Hyperthyroidism likely.
- Weight loss + increased thirst/urination → Diabetes or kidney disease likely.
- Weight loss + vomiting + diarrhea → IBD, parasites, or cancer.
- Weight loss + hiding + lethargy → General illness; vet evaluation needed.

**When to see a vet:** Any unexplained weight loss of more than 10% body weight, or any weight loss accompanied by behavioral changes, warrants prompt veterinary evaluation.

**Common questions:**
- *Why is my cat losing weight but still eating?* — Classic signs of hyperthyroidism, diabetes, or malabsorption disease.
- *Why is my old cat so skinny?* — Senior cats are prone to hyperthyroidism, CKD, and cancer — a vet workup is essential.
- *How do I know if my cat is underweight?* — You should be able to feel ribs with light pressure but not see them prominently; spine and hip bones should not be visible.

## 5. Excessive Thirst and Urination (Polydipsia / Polyuria)

**What it is:** A cat drinking noticeably more water than usual and/or urinating larger amounts more frequently.

**Common causes:**
- Diabetes mellitus.
- Chronic kidney disease (CKD).
- Hyperthyroidism.
- Urinary tract infection (UTI).
- Liver disease.
- Hypercalcemia (elevated blood calcium).
- Psychogenic polydipsia (stress-related, rare).

**Normal water intake reference:** Cats typically drink 60–80 ml of water per kg of body weight per day. Cats on wet food drink significantly less because of moisture content in food.

**When to see a vet:** Any sudden or gradual increase in water consumption and urination requires bloodwork and urinalysis to identify the cause.

**Common questions:**
- *Why is my cat drinking a lot of water all of a sudden?* — Most likely diabetes, kidney disease, or hyperthyroidism — see a vet promptly.
- *Why is my cat peeing so much?* — Often linked to the same conditions as increased drinking; these two symptoms typically occur together.
- *My cat never drinks water — is that normal?* — Cats on wet food naturally drink very little; on dry food only, it can indicate a problem.

## 6. Urinary Issues and Blockages (FLUTD / FUS)

**What it is:** Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease (FLUTD) is an umbrella term for conditions affecting the bladder and urethra. Urinary blockage (obstruction) is a life-threatening emergency.

**Common conditions:**
- Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC) — inflammation with no identifiable cause; stress-linked.
- Urinary crystals or stones (struvite, calcium oxalate).
- Urinary tract infection (UTI) — less common in cats than dogs.
- Urethral obstruction — far more common in male cats due to their narrow urethra.

**Signs of urinary problems:**
- Straining in the litter box.
- Crying or vocalizing while urinating.
- Frequent trips to the litter box with little or no urine produced.
- Blood in urine (hematuria).
- Urinating outside the litter box.
- Licking genitals excessively.

**Signs of urinary blockage (EMERGENCY):**
- Straining with no urine produced for more than 2–4 hours.
- Crying out in pain.
- Lethargy, vomiting, and collapse.
- A hard, distended abdomen.

**When to see a vet:** Any straining with no urine in a male cat is an emergency. Seek care immediately — a blocked cat can die within 24–48 hours.

**Common questions:**
- *Why is my cat straining to urinate?* — Could be crystals, cystitis, or blockage — see a vet, especially if male.
- *Why is there blood in my cat's urine?* — Cystitis, UTI, crystals, or stones — always requires veterinary evaluation.
- *How can I prevent urinary problems in my cat?* — Wet food diet, increased water intake, stress reduction, and urinary health diets prescribed by a vet.
- *Are male cats more prone to urinary blockages?* — Yes, significantly. Male cats have a narrower urethra and are far more likely to experience life-threatening blockages.

## 7. Dental Disease

**What it is:** Periodontal disease, tooth resorption, and gingivitis are among the most prevalent health issues in cats — affecting an estimated 70–80% of cats over age 3.

**Common dental conditions:**
- Gingivitis — inflammation of the gums; earliest and most reversible stage.
- Periodontal disease — infection and destruction of tissues supporting the teeth.
- Tooth resorption (feline odontoclastic resorptive lesions, FORL) — painful breakdown of tooth structure from within.
- Stomatitis — severe, painful inflammation of the entire oral cavity.

**Signs of dental disease:**
- Bad breath (halitosis).
- Drooling, sometimes blood-tinged.
- Pawing at the mouth.
- Dropping food or chewing only on one side.
- Reluctance to eat hard food.
- Weight loss due to pain when eating.

**When to see a vet:** Annual dental checkups are recommended. Any of the above signs warrant a prompt dental evaluation.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat have bad breath?* — Most likely dental disease; less commonly, kidney disease or diabetes.
- *Do cats need their teeth brushed?* — Ideally yes; daily brushing with cat-safe toothpaste is most effective. Start slowly and use positive reinforcement.
- *How often does a cat need a dental cleaning?* — Varies by individual; most cats benefit from professional cleaning every 1–3 years under general anesthesia.
- *What is tooth resorption in cats?* — A painful condition where the tooth structure breaks down from the inside; affected teeth must be extracted.

## 8. Respiratory Issues

**What it is:** Any condition affecting breathing, including sneezing, nasal discharge, wheezing, or labored breathing.

**Common conditions:**
- Upper respiratory infection (URI / "cat cold") — caused by feline herpesvirus (FHV-1) and feline calicivirus (FCV).
- Asthma — chronic inflammatory airway disease.
- Pneumonia — bacterial, viral, or fungal infection of the lungs.
- Pleural effusion — fluid around the lungs.
- Feline infectious peritonitis (FIP) in severe forms.
- Nasal polyps or tumors.

**Signs requiring immediate veterinary care:**
- Open-mouth breathing (cats rarely breathe through their mouths — this is an emergency).
- Breathing with the neck extended and elbows out.
- Blue or purple tinge to gums or tongue (cyanosis).
- Labored or rapid breathing at rest.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat keep sneezing?* — URI, irritant in the environment, dental disease, or nasal polyp. Persistent sneezing with discharge warrants a vet visit.
- *Why does my cat sound congested?* — Likely a respiratory infection (herpesvirus is very common and can recur for life with stress).
- *Does my cat have asthma?* — Signs include recurrent wheezing, coughing, and crouching low with neck outstretched. Diagnosed with X-ray and treated with corticosteroids and bronchodilators.
- *Can cats catch human colds?* — No; feline and human respiratory viruses do not cross species.

## 9. Parasites (Internal and External)

**What it is:** Organisms that live on or inside the cat, causing varying degrees of illness.

**External parasites:**
- Fleas — most common; cause intense itching, flea allergy dermatitis, and transmit tapeworms.
- Ear mites (*Otodectes cynotis*) — cause dark, coffee-ground debris in ears and intense scratching.
- Ticks — can transmit Lyme disease and other pathogens.
- Mange mites (*Notoedres cati*) — cause intense itching and skin crusting.

**Internal parasites:**
- Roundworms (*Toxocara cati*) — most common in kittens; pot-bellied appearance, vomiting, diarrhea.
- Tapeworms (*Dipylidium caninum*) — transmitted via fleas; rice-grain-like segments near tail.
- Hookworms — rare in cats; cause bloody diarrhea and anemia.
- Heartworm (*Dirofilaria immitis*) — transmitted by mosquitoes; can be fatal; there is no approved treatment for cats.
- Giardia and Coccidia — single-celled parasites causing diarrhea.

**Prevention:**
- Year-round flea and tick prevention (topical, oral, or collar-based products).
- Heartworm prevention in endemic areas.
- Regular fecal exams and deworming, especially for outdoor cats.

**Common questions:**
- *How do I know if my cat has fleas?* — Look for "flea dirt" (black specks that turn red on wet paper), excessive scratching, and hair loss.
- *Can indoor cats get parasites?* — Yes; fleas can hitchhike in on clothing or other pets; mosquitoes can enter homes (heartworm risk).
- *What are the white specks near my cat's tail?* — Likely tapeworm segments; requires deworming with praziquantel.
- *Can I get parasites from my cat?* — Some cat parasites are zoonotic (transmissible to humans): Toxocara, hookworm, and ringworm. Practice good hygiene and regular deworming.

## 10. Skin and Coat Problems

**What it is:** Changes in the cat's skin or fur including hair loss, itching, dandruff, lumps, or lesions.

**Common conditions:**
- Flea allergy dermatitis (FAD) — the most common cause of skin disease in cats.
- Environmental or food allergies.
- Ringworm (*Microsporum canis*) — a fungal infection, not a worm; causes circular patches of hair loss.
- Psychogenic alopecia — over-grooming from stress causing bald patches.
- Seborrhea — oily or flaky skin.
- Abscesses — painful fluid-filled swellings, often from cat fight wounds.
- Tumors (mast cell tumors, squamous cell carcinoma).

**Signs that need veterinary attention:**
- Any lump or growth that appears suddenly or changes in size.
- Open sores that do not heal.
- Widespread hair loss or skin redness.
- Intense scratching causing self-injury.

**Common questions:**
- *Why is my cat losing hair?* — Over-grooming (stress), allergies, ringworm, hormonal issues, or parasites.
- *Why does my cat have dandruff?* — Low humidity, poor diet, obesity (can't groom all areas), or skin disease. Check with a vet if persistent.
- *What does ringworm look like on a cat?* — Circular, scaly, hairless patches; can appear anywhere on the body.
- *Why does my cat have a lump under the skin?* — Could be a cyst, abscess, lipoma, or tumor — always have lumps evaluated by a vet.

## 11. Eye Problems

**What it is:** Any abnormality affecting the appearance or function of the eyes.

**Common conditions:**
- Conjunctivitis — red, swollen, or discharging eyes; often caused by FHV-1 or FCV.
- Corneal ulcers — painful scratches or erosions on the eye surface.
- Uveitis — internal eye inflammation; can be linked to FIV, FeLV, FIP, toxoplasmosis.
- Glaucoma — elevated intraocular pressure causing pain and vision loss.
- Cataracts — clouding of the lens; less common in cats than dogs.
- Third eyelid protrusion (haws) — visible nictitating membrane, often indicating illness or dehydration.
- Anisocoria — unequal pupil sizes; can indicate neurological or ocular disease.

**When to see a vet:**
- Any eye discharge that is yellow, green, or thick.
- A closed or squinting eye (sign of pain).
- Cloudiness or color change in the eye.
- Unequal pupil sizes.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat have eye discharge?* — Likely a respiratory infection (herpesvirus); brown discharge can be normal tear staining, green/yellow discharge is not.
- *Why is my cat's third eyelid showing?* — A sign the cat may be unwell, dehydrated, or under stress; persistent protrusion warrants a vet visit.
- *Why are my cat's pupils different sizes?* — Anisocoria can indicate high blood pressure, Horner's syndrome, or neurological issues — requires prompt evaluation.
- *Can cats get pink eye?* — Yes, feline conjunctivitis is very common and often linked to herpesvirus.

## 12. Ear Problems

**What it is:** Conditions affecting the outer ear canal or middle/inner ear.

**Common conditions:**
- Ear mites — very common, especially in kittens and outdoor cats; cause dark debris and intense scratching.
- Otitis externa — bacterial or yeast infection of the outer ear canal.
- Polyps — benign tissue growths in the ear canal.
- Hematoma — blood pooling in the ear flap (pinna) from shaking or scratching.
- Deafness — white cats with blue eyes have a higher genetic predisposition to congenital deafness.

**Signs of ear problems:**
- Frequent head shaking.
- Scratching at ears.
- Dark brown or black debris in the ear canal.
- Odor from the ear.
- Head tilt (suggests inner ear involvement).
- Loss of balance or circling.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat scratch its ears so much?* — Ear mites, infection, or polyp; examination of the ear canal is needed.
- *What is the dark stuff in my cat's ears?* — If waxy and dark brown, could be normal cerumen; coffee-ground texture strongly suggests ear mites.
- *Why is my cat shaking its head repeatedly?* — Ear discomfort from mites, infection, foreign body, or polyp.
- *Why does my cat have a swollen ear flap?* — An aural hematoma, usually caused by vigorous head shaking; requires veterinary drainage.

## 13. Kidney Disease (Chronic Kidney Disease — CKD)

**What it is:** Progressive, irreversible loss of kidney function. CKD is one of the leading causes of illness and death in older cats.

**Prevalence:** Affects approximately 30–40% of cats over the age of 10.

**Stages (IRIS staging system):**
- Stage 1: Minimal loss of function; often no symptoms.
- Stage 2: Mild loss; subtle symptoms begin (slight weight loss, increased thirst).
- Stage 3: Moderate loss; more pronounced symptoms, management becomes critical.
- Stage 4: Severe loss; poor prognosis without aggressive management.

**Common symptoms:**
- Increased thirst and urination.
- Weight loss and muscle wasting.
- Decreased appetite.
- Vomiting and nausea.
- Lethargy.
- Poor coat quality.
- Bad breath (ammonia or uremic smell).

**Management:**
- Prescription kidney diet (low phosphorus, moderate protein).
- Subcutaneous fluids at home or at the clinic.
- Phosphorus binders.
- Blood pressure medication if hypertensive.
- Anti-nausea medication.
- Regular monitoring (every 3–6 months).

**Common questions:**
- *What are the early signs of kidney disease in cats?* — Increased drinking and urination, subtle weight loss, and decreased appetite.
- *How long can a cat live with kidney disease?* — Varies widely by stage; cats in early stages can live years with proper management; late-stage may be months.
- *What should I feed a cat with kidney disease?* — A veterinary prescription renal diet; do not change diet without vet guidance.
- *Can kidney disease in cats be reversed?* — No; CKD is irreversible, but progression can be slowed significantly with treatment.

## 14. Diabetes Mellitus

**What it is:** A metabolic disorder in which the body cannot properly regulate blood glucose due to insulin deficiency or resistance.

**Types in cats:**
- Type 2 (most common in cats) — insulin resistance, often associated with obesity.
- Type 1 — insufficient insulin production; less common.

**Risk factors:** Obesity, male sex, middle to older age, indoor sedentary lifestyle, certain medications (corticosteroids).

**Symptoms:**
- Increased thirst and urination.
- Increased appetite with weight loss.
- Lethargy and weakness.
- Hind limb weakness or "plantigrade stance" (walking on the hocks) — a late sign of diabetic neuropathy.

**Treatment:**
- Insulin injections (typically twice daily).
- Low-carbohydrate, high-protein wet food diet.
- Weight management.
- Regular blood glucose monitoring.
- Some cats achieve diabetic remission with early, aggressive management.

**Common questions:**
- *How do I know if my cat has diabetes?* — Increased thirst, urination, appetite with weight loss; confirmed via bloodwork and urinalysis.
- *Can cat diabetes be cured?* — Remission is possible, especially in early-diagnosed cats on a strict diet and insulin protocol.
- *What do I feed a diabetic cat?* — High-protein, low-carbohydrate wet food; avoid dry kibble.
- *How do I give my cat insulin?* — Your vet will train you; injections are typically given subcutaneously (under the skin) at the scruff or flank with a fine needle.

## 15. Hyperthyroidism

**What it is:** Overproduction of thyroid hormones by an enlarged or hyperactive thyroid gland. The most common endocrine disorder in older cats.

**Prevalence:** Affects approximately 10% of cats over age 10.

**Symptoms:**
- Weight loss despite increased appetite (often ravenous hunger).
- Hyperactivity, restlessness, and vocalizing.
- Increased thirst and urination.
- Vomiting and diarrhea.
- Poor, unkempt coat.
- Rapid or irregular heartbeat.

**Treatment options:**
- Methimazole medication (daily pill or ear gel) — most common; controls but does not cure.
- Radioactive iodine (I-131) therapy — curative; the gold standard treatment.
- Surgical thyroidectomy — effective but less commonly performed.
- Prescription iodine-restricted diet (Hill's y/d) — controls but requires exclusive feeding.

**Important:** Hyperthyroidism can mask underlying kidney disease; thyroid treatment may "unmask" CKD. Kidney function should be monitored after starting treatment.

**Common questions:**
- *What are the signs of hyperthyroidism in cats?* — Weight loss with increased appetite, hyperactivity, increased thirst, vomiting.
- *How is hyperthyroidism diagnosed?* — Blood test measuring total T4 (thyroxine) levels.
- *Is hyperthyroidism curable in cats?* — Yes, radioactive iodine therapy is curative in over 95% of cases.
- *Why is my old cat always hungry but losing weight?* — Classic presentation of hyperthyroidism; see a vet for bloodwork.

## 16. Obesity

**What it is:** Excess body fat accumulation resulting in body weight 20% or more above ideal. Obesity is the most common nutritional disorder in domestic cats.

**Prevalence:** Estimated 30–40% of pet cats in developed countries are overweight or obese.

**Health risks associated with obesity:**
- Diabetes mellitus.
- Osteoarthritis and joint pain.
- Hepatic lipidosis.
- Urinary tract disease.
- Respiratory difficulty.
- Reduced immune function.
- Decreased life expectancy.

**Body condition scoring (BCS):** Vets use a 1–9 scale. Ideal is 4–5 out of 9. A score of 6–7 is overweight; 8–9 is obese.

**Weight management:**
- Measured portions — eliminate free-feeding of dry food.
- High-protein, low-carbohydrate wet food.
- Puzzle feeders to slow eating and increase activity.
- Interactive play — minimum 15–20 minutes daily.
- Veterinary weight loss diets if needed.
- Rule out underlying thyroid or metabolic conditions first.

**Common questions:**
- *How can I tell if my cat is overweight?* — You should easily feel ribs without pressing hard; if you cannot, the cat is likely overweight.
- *How do I help my cat lose weight safely?* — Gradual calorie reduction (no more than 1–2% body weight loss per week); rapid weight loss causes hepatic lipidosis.
- *Can neutering cause weight gain in cats?* — Yes; neutering reduces metabolic rate by approximately 20–30%; adjust food portions accordingly.
- *What is the ideal weight for a cat?* — Varies by breed; most domestic shorthair cats have an ideal weight of 3.5–5 kg (8–11 lbs).

## 17. Arthritis and Joint Disease

**What it is:** Degenerative joint disease (osteoarthritis) causing chronic pain, inflammation, and reduced mobility. Significantly underdiagnosed in cats.

**Prevalence:** Studies suggest over 90% of cats over age 12 show radiographic evidence of arthritis.

**Why it is underdiagnosed in cats:** Cats rarely limp or vocalize pain from arthritis. Instead they subtly reduce activity, which owners often mistake for normal aging.

**Signs:**
- Reduced jumping height or reluctance to jump.
- Difficulty accessing the litter box (may go outside the box).
- Decreased grooming (stiff joints make it harder to reach spots).
- Unkempt coat or matted fur.
- Reduced activity and increased sleeping.
- Irritability when touched in certain areas.
- Muscle wasting in hind limbs.

**Management:**
- Veterinary-prescribed pain management (NSAIDs formulated for cats, gabapentin, buprenorphine).
- Monoclonal antibody therapy (frunevetmab/Solensia) — a newer, effective option.
- Environmental modifications: ramps, low-sided litter boxes, raised food bowls, heated beds.
- Joint supplements (omega-3 fatty acids, glucosamine; evidence is moderate).
- Weight management to reduce joint stress.
- Physical therapy in some cases.

**Common questions:**
- *How do I know if my cat has arthritis?* — Reduced jumping, reluctance to use stairs, missing the litter box, and reduced grooming are the key signs.
- *Can cats take human pain relievers for arthritis?* — No. Ibuprofen, acetaminophen, and aspirin are toxic to cats. Only use vet-prescribed medications.
- *What can I do at home to help my arthritic cat?* — Provide ramps, low-entry litter boxes, warm soft bedding, and minimize the need to jump.

## 18. Cancer

**What it is:** Uncontrolled cell growth forming malignant or benign tumors. Cancer is a leading cause of death in older cats.

**Common cancers in cats:**
- Lymphoma — the most common cancer in cats; can affect intestines, chest, or other organs.
- Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — often on the nose, ears, or mouth; strongly linked to sun exposure in light-colored cats.
- Mammary (breast) cancer — aggressive in cats; approximately 85–90% of mammary tumors are malignant.
- Fibrosarcoma — often at injection sites (vaccine-associated sarcoma).
- Mast cell tumors — can occur on skin or in internal organs.

**General warning signs:**
- Unexplained weight loss.
- Lumps or swellings that grow or change.
- Non-healing sores.
- Difficulty eating, swallowing, or breathing.
- Bleeding or unusual discharge.
- Persistent lameness.
- Decreased appetite and lethargy.

**Important prevention notes:**
- Spaying female cats before their first heat dramatically reduces mammary cancer risk.
- Limiting sun exposure protects against SCC in white or light-colored cats.

**Common questions:**
- *What are the signs of cancer in cats?* — Weight loss, lumps, lethargy, appetite loss, and non-healing wounds are common indicators.
- *Is cancer common in cats?* — Yes; cancer accounts for approximately 32% of deaths in cats over age 10.
- *How is cancer treated in cats?* — Surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and palliative care depending on cancer type and stage; cats generally tolerate chemotherapy better than humans.
- *What is vaccine-associated sarcoma in cats?* — A rare but aggressive tumor that can develop at injection sites; modern vaccination protocols minimize risk.

## 19. Vaccinations

**What it is:** Immunizations that protect cats against infectious diseases by stimulating the immune system.

**Core vaccines (recommended for all cats):**
- FVRCP (Feline Viral Rhinotracheitis, Calicivirus, Panleukopenia) — sometimes called the "feline distemper" combo.
- Rabies — required by law in most jurisdictions.

**Non-core vaccines (based on lifestyle and risk):**
- FeLV (Feline Leukemia Virus) — recommended for outdoor cats and cats in multi-cat households.
- FIV — available in some countries; less commonly used.
- Bordetella, Chlamydia — for high-risk environments like shelters.

**Standard schedule:**
- Kittens: Series starting at 6–8 weeks, every 3–4 weeks until 16 weeks of age.
- Adults: Boosters at 1 year; then every 1–3 years depending on vaccine and lifestyle.

**Common questions:**
- *Do indoor cats need vaccines?* — Yes; core vaccines (FVRCP and Rabies) are recommended for all cats regardless of lifestyle.
- *Can vaccines make my cat sick?* — Mild lethargy and soreness for 1–2 days is normal. Severe reactions (facial swelling, vomiting, collapse) are rare but require immediate veterinary care.
- *How often does my cat need vaccines?* — Core vaccines are often given every 3 years after initial boosters; rabies frequency varies by local law and product.
- *What is panleukopenia?* — A highly contagious and often fatal viral disease (similar to parvovirus in dogs); prevented by the FVRCP vaccine.

## 20. Spaying and Neutering

**What it is:** Surgical removal of the reproductive organs. Spaying (ovariohysterectomy) in females; neutering (orchidectomy/castration) in males.

**Benefits:**
- Eliminates risk of uterine infection (pyometra) — a life-threatening emergency in intact females.
- Dramatically reduces mammary cancer risk if done before first heat.
- Eliminates testicular cancer in males.
- Reduces or eliminates spraying, roaming, and aggression.
- Prevents unwanted litters.

**Recommended age:** Traditionally 5–6 months; many shelters perform pediatric spay/neuter at 8 weeks. Discuss timing with your vet.

**Pyometra (intact females):** A severe uterine infection that can be fatal if untreated; signs include lethargy, increased thirst, vaginal discharge, and distended abdomen. Requires emergency spay.

**Common questions:**
- *At what age should I spay or neuter my cat?* — Typically 5–6 months, before the first heat cycle; consult your vet.
- *Will neutering change my cat's personality?* — It reduces hormonally driven behaviors (aggression, roaming, spraying) but does not change core personality.
- *Can an older cat be spayed or neutered?* — Yes; while older cats carry slightly higher anesthetic risk, the procedure is still routinely performed and recommended.
- *What is pyometra?* — A life-threatening uterine infection in unspayed females requiring emergency surgery.

## 21. Toxins and Poisonous Substances

**What it is:** Substances that are toxic or fatal to cats when ingested, inhaled, or absorbed through the skin.

**Common toxins:**
- **Plants:** Lilies (*Lilium* and *Hemerocallis* species) — even small amounts are acutely fatal to cats causing kidney failure. Also toxic: sago palm, tulip bulbs, azalea, dieffenbachia, pothos.
- **Human medications:** Acetaminophen (Tylenol) — highly toxic; destroys red blood cells and liver. Ibuprofen, aspirin, antidepressants, ADHD medications.
- **Household chemicals:** Bleach, ammonia, phenol-based cleaners (Lysol), ethylene glycol (antifreeze).
- **Foods:** Onions and garlic (all forms), grapes and raisins, xylitol (artificial sweetener), alcohol, chocolate, macadamia nuts.
- **Essential oils:** Tea tree oil, peppermint oil, eucalyptus, cinnamon, citrus — toxic especially when applied to skin or diffused in poorly ventilated areas. Cats lack the liver enzyme to metabolize these compounds.
- **Flea/tick products formulated for dogs:** Permethrin is safe for dogs but highly toxic to cats.

**Signs of poisoning:**
- Drooling, vomiting, diarrhea.
- Tremors or seizures.
- Difficulty breathing.
- Dilated pupils.
- Collapse or sudden extreme lethargy.

**What to do:**
1. Do not induce vomiting unless specifically instructed by a vet — it can worsen some poisoning.
2. Contact your veterinarian, emergency animal hospital, or poison control immediately.
3. ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (US): 888-426-4435 (consultation fee may apply).
4. Bring the product or plant to the vet if possible.

**Common questions:**
- *Are lilies really that dangerous for cats?* — Yes; all parts of true lilies and daylilies are acutely fatal — even pollen or water from a vase can cause kidney failure.
- *My cat licked a Lysol-cleaned surface — is that dangerous?* — Phenol-containing products are toxic to cats. Rinse the area and monitor; call a vet.
- *Are essential oil diffusers safe around cats?* — Passive diffusers in well-ventilated rooms pose lower risk but direct application or reed diffusers near cats should be avoided entirely.
- *Can cats eat tuna?* — Occasional tuna is fine; a tuna-only diet can cause thiamine deficiency and mercury accumulation over time.

## 22. Senior Cat Health (Cats 10+ Years)

**What it is:** Age-related health changes and the most common conditions affecting senior and geriatric cats.

**Life stage definitions:**
- Mature: 7–10 years.
- Senior: 11–14 years.
- Geriatric: 15+ years.

**Most common health issues in senior cats:**
- Chronic kidney disease (CKD).
- Hyperthyroidism.
- Hypertension (high blood pressure) — often secondary to CKD or hyperthyroidism.
- Osteoarthritis.
- Dental disease.
- Diabetes mellitus.
- Cancer.
- Feline cognitive dysfunction syndrome (FCD) — similar to dementia in humans.

**Signs of feline cognitive dysfunction:**
- Disorientation and confusion.
- Yowling at night.
- Changes in sleep-wake cycle.
- House soiling in a previously trained cat.
- Decreased interaction with family.
- Staring into space.

**Recommended veterinary care for senior cats:** Bi-annual checkups (every 6 months) with bloodwork, urinalysis, and blood pressure measurement starting at age 10.

**Common questions:**
- *How old is my cat in human years?* — A 10-year-old cat is roughly equivalent to a 56-year-old human; each cat year after age 2 ≈ approximately 4 human years.
- *Why is my old cat yowling at night?* — Cognitive dysfunction, hyperthyroidism, pain, or hypertension; all are treatable — see a vet.
- *What is normal aging vs. disease in senior cats?* — Slowing down is normal; significant weight loss, vomiting, increased thirst, or confusion are not — they signal disease.
- *How often should a senior cat see the vet?* — Every 6 months; many serious conditions in senior cats are caught earlier and managed better with twice-yearly monitoring.
