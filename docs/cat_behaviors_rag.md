## 1. Kneading ("Making Biscuits")

**What it is:** Cats rhythmically push their paws in and out against a soft surface, alternating left and right.

**Why cats do it:**
- Originates from kittenhood — kittens knead their mother's belly to stimulate milk flow.
- In adult cats it is a self-soothing behavior associated with comfort and contentment.
- Cats may knead on blankets, pillows, laps, or other soft surfaces.
- It can also mark territory, as cats have scent glands in their paw pads.

**Is it normal?** Yes. Kneading is a healthy, instinctive behavior. Excessive kneading paired with suckling fabric may indicate early weaning stress.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat knead me?* — Your cat feels safe and bonded with you.
- *Why does my cat knead and drool?* — Deep relaxation; the cat is re-experiencing the comfort of nursing.
- *Should I stop my cat from kneading?* — Generally no, though you can redirect to a blanket if claws are an issue.

## 2. Slow Blinking

**What it is:** A cat partially or fully closes its eyes slowly while looking at a person or another animal.

**Why cats do it:**
- Communicates trust and non-aggression. In cat social language, closing eyes around another being signals you are not a threat.
- Often called a "cat kiss" or "eye kiss."
- Research (Humphrey et al., 2020, *Scientific Reports*) confirmed that cats respond positively to humans who slow-blink back at them.

**How to respond:** Slow-blink back at your cat to strengthen your bond. Avoid staring without blinking, which cats read as threatening.

**Common questions:**
- *What does it mean when a cat slow-blinks at you?* — It is a sign of affection and trust.
- *Can I slow-blink at my cat?* — Yes, and cats are more likely to approach you afterward.

## 3. Bringing "Gifts" (Dead or Live Prey)

**What it is:** Cats bring dead animals (birds, mice, insects) or toys to their owners.

**Why cats do it:**
- Cats are instinctive hunters. Bringing prey home is a natural behavior.
- Female cats in the wild bring prey to teach kittens to hunt. Your cat may be "teaching" you.
- Some behaviorists suggest cats see their owner as a poor hunter and are trying to help.
- Cats may also simply be proud of their catch and want to share.

**Is it a problem?** Not behaviorally — it is a compliment. To reduce it, keep cats indoors or use a brightly colored breakaway collar with a bell.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat bring me dead animals?* — Instinct, pride, and possibly a nurturing impulse toward you.
- *Why does my cat bring me toys?* — Same instinct expressed with a safe substitute.

## 4. Chattering / Chirping at Birds and Prey

**What it is:** A rapid, stuttering jaw movement accompanied by a "ck-ck-ck" or chirping vocalization, usually directed at birds or insects through a window.

**Why cats do it:**
- Thought to be a response to frustrated predatory excitement — the cat sees prey it cannot reach.
- Some researchers propose it mimics the kill bite used to sever the spine of prey.
- Others suggest it is an involuntary response driven by the predatory arousal state.

**Is it harmful?** No. It is normal predatory behavior. It does indicate your cat wants more environmental enrichment or playtime.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat make a chirping noise at birds?* — Predatory excitement and possible frustration.
- *Is chattering a sign my cat is stressed?* — Not typically; it is more excitement than stress.

## 5. Midnight Zoomies (Frenetic Random Activity Periods — FRAPs)

**What it is:** Sudden explosive bursts of running, jumping, and wild activity, often late at night.

**Why cats do it:**
- Cats are crepuscular (most active at dawn and dusk). Indoor cats may shift activity to nighttime.
- FRAPs are a way to release pent-up energy after long periods of rest.
- Can also follow using the litter box — possibly a reflexive relief response.
- Kittens and young cats experience FRAPs more frequently.

**How to manage it:**
- Play with your cat for 15–20 minutes before your bedtime to tire them out.
- Provide puzzle feeders and enrichment toys for daytime stimulation.
- A second cat can provide a playmate to absorb excess energy.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat run around like crazy at night?* — Crepuscular instincts and pent-up energy.
- *Are zoomies dangerous?* — Rarely, though ensure the environment is safe (no fragile items to knock over).

## 6. Head Bunting and Rubbing (Bunting)

**What it is:** A cat presses or rubs its forehead, cheeks, or chin against a person, object, or other animal.

**Why cats do it:**
- Cats have scent glands on their cheeks, forehead, and chin. Bunting deposits their scent — a form of marking and claiming ownership in a positive, social way.
- Signals trust, affection, and social bonding.
- Cats bunt other cats they are bonded with; bunting a human is a sign of inclusion in their social group.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat headbutt me?* — Affection and scent-marking you as "theirs."
- *Why does my cat rub its face on everything?* — Territorial scent-marking and environmental familiarity.
- *Why does my cat rub its face on my phone/laptop?* — The same scent-marking instinct, often also seeking your attention.

## 7. Hiding Behavior

**What it is:** A cat retreats to enclosed, dark, or elevated spaces and remains there for extended periods.

**Why cats do it (normal reasons):**
- Natural instinct — cats are both predator and prey in the wild; hiding is a safety strategy.
- Rest and sleep in a secure, quiet space.
- Processing a new environment, new people, or new pets in the home.
- Reacting to loud noises (thunderstorms, fireworks).

**Why cats do it (concern reasons):**
- Illness or pain — sick cats instinctively hide. If a normally social cat suddenly hides for more than 24–48 hours, consult a vet.
- Stress or fear from a significant change in the home.

**Common questions:**
- *Why is my cat suddenly hiding?* — Could be stress or illness; monitor for other symptoms.
- *Is it normal for cats to hide all day?* — Some shy cats do; sudden changes in hiding behavior warrant attention.
- *How do I get my cat to stop hiding?* — Provide safe spaces, reduce stressors, and give the cat time to acclimate.

## 8. Knocking Things Off Tables

**What it is:** Cats deliberately push objects off elevated surfaces with their paws.

**Why cats do it:**
- Predatory instinct — cats use their paws to test whether objects are alive or interesting prey.
- Attention-seeking behavior; if a human reacts every time, the cat learns this is effective.
- Curiosity and sensory exploration.
- Boredom and under-stimulation.

**How to manage it:**
- Increase interactive play sessions.
- Provide puzzle toys and enrichment.
- Avoid reacting dramatically, which reinforces the behavior.

**Common questions:**
- *Why do cats knock things off tables?* — Instinct, curiosity, and often for your attention.
- *How do I stop my cat from pushing things off surfaces?* — Enrichment and ignoring the behavior removes the reward.

## 9. Excessive Vocalization / Meowing

**What it is:** Cats meow frequently, loudly, or at unusual hours.

**Why cats do it:**
- Adult cats primarily meow to communicate with humans (not other cats). It is a learned behavior directed at people.
- Hunger, wanting attention, or asking to go outside.
- Older cats may vocalize more due to cognitive decline (feline cognitive dysfunction) or sensory loss.
- Unspayed females in heat yowl loudly and persistently.
- Pain or illness can cause increased vocalization.
- Breed predisposition — Siamese and Oriental breeds are naturally more vocal.

**When to see a vet:** Sudden increase in vocalization, especially in senior cats, warrants a veterinary check for hyperthyroidism, hypertension, or cognitive dysfunction.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat meow so much?* — Attention, hunger, breed temperament, or a medical issue.
- *Why does my cat meow at night?* — Crepuscular activity cycle, hunger, or age-related cognitive changes.
- *Why does my cat meow at nothing?* — Possibly tracking sounds/smells humans can't detect, or cognitive changes in older cats.

## 10. Rolling Over and Exposing the Belly

**What it is:** A cat rolls onto its back and shows its abdomen.

**Why cats do it:**
- Signals trust and comfort — the belly is the most vulnerable part of a cat's body.
- An invitation to interact, though not always a request to be touched there.
- Part of play behavior, especially in younger cats.
- Stretching and self-grooming.

**The "belly trap":** Most cats do not actually enjoy belly rubs despite showing their belly. The exposed belly is a sign of trust, not necessarily an invitation for touch. Petting the belly may trigger a defensive bite or scratch reflex.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat show me its belly but then bite me when I touch it?* — The belly display is trust, not a request for touch.
- *Why does my cat roll around on the floor?* — Contentment, play, or scent-rolling on an interesting smell.

## 11. Trilling

**What it is:** A short, high-pitched, rolling "brrr" or "prr" sound, distinct from a meow or purr.

**Why cats do it:**
- Mother cats trill to communicate with kittens — a soft "follow me" or "pay attention" signal.
- Adult cats direct trills at humans they are bonded with, often as a greeting or to get attention.
- Commonly used when a cat is happy to see you or wants you to follow them somewhere.

**Common questions:**
- *What does it mean when a cat trills?* — A friendly greeting or an invitation to follow and interact.
- *Is trilling the same as meowing?* — No; trills are shorter, higher, and more musical — a distinctly social sound.

## 12. Sitting on Your Belongings / In Boxes

**What it is:** Cats sit in boxes, bags, open suitcases, on laptops, books, or anything you are currently using.

**Why cats do it (boxes and enclosures):**
- Enclosed spaces make cats feel secure — a survival instinct for ambush predators.
- Boxes reduce stress; research (Arhant et al.) found shelter cats given boxes showed lower stress levels and adapted faster.

**Why cats do it (your belongings):**
- Your scent is on those items, making them attractive.
- Attention-seeking — you are focused on the item, so sitting on it gets your attention.
- Warmth (laptops especially).

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat sit in boxes?* — Security, warmth, and natural ambush predator instinct.
- *Why does my cat sit on my laptop?* — Warmth, your scent, and competition for your attention.
- *Why do cats love boxes?* — Enclosed spaces trigger a sense of safety and control over their environment.

## 13. Purring

**What it is:** A continuous, low-frequency vibrating sound produced during inhalation and exhalation.

**Why cats do it:**
- Contentment and relaxation — the most common reason.
- Self-soothing during stress, illness, or injury. Cats often purr at the vet.
- Communication with humans — cats have developed a "solicitation purr" that embeds a higher-frequency cry to signal urgency (e.g., hunger).
- Healing — purr frequencies (25–150 Hz) may promote bone density and tissue healing.

**Common questions:**
- *Why do cats purr?* — Primarily contentment, but also self-comfort and communication.
- *Why does my cat purr when I pet them but then bite me?* — Overstimulation; the purr indicates initial pleasure, the bite signals "enough."
- *Do cats purr when they are scared or hurt?* — Yes, purring can be a self-soothing mechanism in stress or pain.

## 14. Grooming and Over-Grooming

**What it is:** Cats spend 30–50% of their waking hours grooming. Over-grooming results in bald patches or skin irritation.

**Why cats groom (normal):**
- Coat maintenance — removes loose fur, dirt, and parasites.
- Temperature regulation.
- Social bonding — cats groom other cats and sometimes people they are bonded with (allogrooming).
- Stress displacement — a brief grooming session after an awkward moment is a calming behavior.

**Why cats over-groom:**
- Stress, anxiety, or boredom — psychogenic alopecia.
- Skin conditions, allergies, or parasites (fleas, mites).
- Pain in the area being over-groomed.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat groom me?* — Social bonding; you are part of their social group.
- *Why is my cat losing fur from grooming too much?* — Likely stress or a skin condition — consult a vet.
- *Why does my cat groom after I pet it?* — Redistributing scent and re-establishing its own scent profile.

## 15. Staring Without Blinking

**What it is:** A cat holds prolonged, unblinking eye contact with a person or another animal.

**Why cats do it:**
- Direct, unblinking stare is a dominance or threat signal in cat communication.
- Directed at prey — intense predatory focus.
- Directed at humans who are unfamiliar — assessment or wariness.
- With bonded humans, staring can simply be attention-seeking or curiosity.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat stare at me?* — Could be curiosity, attention-seeking, or waiting for you to do something interesting.
- *Is it bad to stare at a cat?* — Unblinking stares can feel threatening to a cat; slow-blink to soften your gaze.
- *Why does my cat stare at the wall?* — Cats detect sounds and vibrations humans cannot (insects in walls, etc.); rarely a concern.

## 16. Eating Grass and Plants

**What it is:** Cats chew on grass, houseplants, or other vegetation despite being obligate carnivores.

**Why cats do it:**
- Grass may help cats regurgitate indigestible material (fur, feathers) from their stomachs.
- May provide trace micronutrients or folic acid.
- The texture may be appealing for chewing instinct.
- Boredom or dietary deficiency in some cases.

**Safety note:** Many common houseplants are toxic to cats (lilies, pothos, philodendron). Provide cat-safe grass (wheatgrass, oat grass) as an alternative.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat eat grass and then vomit?* — Intentional self-induced regurgitation to clear the stomach.
- *Is it safe for cats to eat grass?* — Plain grass yes; many houseplants no — check ASPCA toxic plant lists.

## 17. Territorial Spraying

**What it is:** A cat backs up to a vertical surface, raises its tail, and sprays urine.

**Why cats do it:**
- Scent-marking to establish territory, especially in multi-cat households or when detecting outside cats.
- Stress response to changes in environment, routine, or household members.
- Unaltered cats spray as part of reproductive communication.

**Difference from urinating outside the litter box:** Spraying is a small amount on a vertical surface; inappropriate elimination is a larger amount on a horizontal surface and often signals a litter box or medical issue.

**Management:**
- Spay/neuter eliminates or greatly reduces spraying in most cats.
- Enzymatic cleaners remove scent fully.
- Feliway (synthetic pheromone) diffusers can reduce stress-based spraying.

**Common questions:**
- *Why is my neutered cat still spraying?* — Stress or habit; neutering reduces but doesn't always eliminate spraying.
- *Why is my cat spraying inside?* — Territorial stress, new animals, or changes in the home.

## 18. Loafing (The Loaf Position)

**What it is:** A cat sits with all four paws tucked under its body, resembling a loaf of bread.

**Why cats do it:**
- Conserves body heat by minimizing surface area exposure.
- A resting position that allows quick response — paws are ready to spring into action.
- Indicates a calm but alert state (not deeply asleep).

**What it signals about mood:** A loafing cat is typically relaxed and comfortable. A tight loaf with squinted eyes often means mild discomfort or nausea — context matters.

**Common questions:**
- *Why does my cat sit like a loaf?* — Warmth and comfort; it is a relaxed but alert resting posture.
- *Is the loaf position a sign of pain?* — A very tight loaf with hunched posture can indicate discomfort; look for other signs.
